What this lecture is really about

Everything up to now gave you shape: r = p/(1 + e cos θ) tells you where the satellite is if you already know θ. Nobody hands you θ. What you actually have is a clock.

So the real question is: given a time, where is it? And the honest answer is that no closed-form function θ(t) exists for an ellipse. Kepler's equation is the workaround, and it is transcendental — E appears both alone and inside a sine, so it cannot be isolated.

That single fact is why this lecture exists, why MATLAB's fzero shows up in the slides, and why the professor said "do not use" the integral form on slide 3.

Why E was invented — the part the slides skip

Kepler's 2nd law says area sweeps out uniformly. So if you can write the swept area as a function of an angle, that angle is linear in time. The trick: an ellipse is just a circle squashed vertically by the factor b/a, and squashing scales every area by the same b/a.

On the circle, the area from periapsis out to angle E is a sector minus a triangle:

A_circle = ½a²E − ½(ae)(a sin E) = (a²/2)(E − e sin E)

Squash it, and A = (ab/2)(E − e sin E). Since area is proportional to time, A/(πab) = t/T, which rearranges to

n t = E − e sin E

That is Kepler's equation, and now the strange −e sin E has a meaning: it is exactly the triangle you subtract. E is the angle that makes area — and therefore time — come out linear. θ never could.

The chain, and which way is hard

Three quantities, two links. The middle link is the only hard one.

t → M = n t → [Kepler] → E → [half-angle] → θ → r, v

State → time is easy. From r and v you get ε, then a, then the eccentricity vector, then θ by atan2, then E, then t = (E − e sin E)/n. Every step is explicit. Slides 6–8.

Time → state is hard. Going the other way you hit E − e sin E = M and must iterate. Slides 9–10. This asymmetry is the point of the whole lecture, and it is exactly the kind of thing an exam asks you to recognise.

Solving it by hand — Newton

fzero is fine for homework, but know the method. Newton's iteration on f(E) = E − e sin E − M is

E ← E − (E − e sin E − M) / (1 − e cos E)

Start at E₀ = M + e sin M rather than M — it typically saves an iteration or two. Convergence is quadratic and usually done in three or four steps for e below about 0.8. Watch the iteration counter above as you raise e: near e = 1 the denominator 1 − e cos E approaches zero at periapsis and the method starts to struggle. That is a real failure mode in comet and transfer-orbit work.

Things that will cost you marks

Use atan2, never atan. The professor flags the quadrant check twice. The half-angle form θ = 2 arctan[√((1+e)/(1−e)) tan(E/2)] behaves because the factor of 2 outside puts the result in the right half-plane — but code it as a two-argument atan2 anyway.

t is time since periapsis passage, not time since the epoch you were given. If t exceeds one period, subtract whole periods first, or wrap M into [0, 2π).

Radians. Kepler's equation mixes E, which must be in radians, with sin E. Degrees will silently give you a wrong answer rather than an error.

a is negative for hyperbolas, so n = √(μ/a³) is not real there. Hyperbolic orbits use a different anomaly (F, with sinh) and a different Kepler equation. This lecture is ellipses only — check which one the problem wants.

Know the geometric identities. r = a(1 − e cos E) is the fastest route to range, and x = a(cos E − e), y = b sin E gives position directly in the perifocal frame without touching θ at all.

PQW, briefly

Every formula on these slides lives in the perifocal frame: P points at periapsis, Q sits 90° ahead in the orbit plane, W runs along the angular momentum vector. It is the frame where the orbit is flat and the algebra is clean — the z component of position is identically zero, which is why the slide-10 vectors have a 0 in the third slot.

Converting PQW to an Earth-fixed or inertial frame is a separate rotation, and it is almost certainly the next lecture.
