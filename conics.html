Why the tilt is the whole story

A generator — one of the straight lines on the cone's surface — leans α away from the axis. Hold the plane horizontal and it meets every generator at the same distance, so the section closes into a circle.

Tilt the plane and it starts biting shallow on one side and deep on the other. The section stretches. That stretch is exactly what e measures:

e = sin φ / cos α

At φ = 90° − α the plane runs parallel to one generator. It can never meet that line, so the section cannot close, and you get a parabola. Tilt further and the plane reaches the opposite nappe as well — that second intersection is the second branch of the hyperbola.

Every boundary is the same test: is α + φ less than, equal to, or greater than 90°?

Where the formula comes from

Put the apex at the origin with the axis along y. The cone is x² + z² = (y tan α)², and the tilted plane is y = h₀ + z tan φ. Substituting one into the other and rewriting in coordinates on the plane itself gives

u² + (cos²φ − tan²α sin²φ) v² + … = 0

which is an ellipse when the v² coefficient is positive, a hyperbola when negative. Completing the square gives semi-axes a and b with

e² = 1 − b²/a² = sin²φ / cos²α

So the eccentricity falls straight out of the two angles, and h₀ never appears. That is why sliding the plane up and down rescales the section without ever changing its shape.

Why a focus is a focus

Drop in a sphere that is inscribed in the cone and tangent to the cutting plane. For an ellipse there are two — one on each side. Each one touches the plane at exactly one point, and that point is a focus.

The proof is short. From any point P on the section, all tangent lengths to a given sphere are equal. Running along the generator through P, the distance from P to each sphere's contact circle is that same tangent length. Their sum is the length of generator between the two contact circles — a constant, independent of P. Constant sum of distances to two points is the definition of an ellipse.

Turn the near sphere on and watch the amber dot: it is the tangency point, and it lands precisely on the focus in the face-on view.

The bridge to orbits

The orbit equation from the R2BP derivation is the polar equation of a conic with the focus at the origin:

r = (h²/μ) / (1 + e cos θ) = p / (1 + e cos θ)

Because every solution of the two-body problem takes that form, an orbit cannot be anything but a conic section. The only remaining question is what e happens to be — and energy answers it:

e = √(1 + 2εh²/μ²), ε = v²/2 − μ/r

ε < 0 gives e < 1 and a bound orbit. ε = 0 gives exactly e = 1, escape with nothing to spare. ε > 0 gives e > 1 and a hyperbolic escape with velocity left over.

At periapsis vp = √(1+e) · vcirc, so speeding up from a circular orbit by a factor of √2 walks e from 0 to 1 — the instant the plane goes parallel to a generator. Tilting the plane and burning the engine are the same operation.
